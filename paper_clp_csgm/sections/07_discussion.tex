\section{Interpretation, scope, and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

CLP-CSGM uses three pieces that act together. The decoder range
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{orig}}$ is the set of target windows, in the
original units, that the decoder can produce.
The conditional map predicts $\boldsymbol{z}_0=h(\boldsymbol{u})$ from the
dense logs. This code initializes the first latent trajectory and remains the
center of the quadratic penalty. The measurement term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:clp_csgm_objective} penalizes
disagreement with the sparse observations in the original target units and
does not force those observations to be met exactly. The decoder range limits which windows can
appear. The code $\boldsymbol{z}_0$ carries the dense logs into that search,
and the measurement term uses the sparse target values. Unlike direct
$[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{b}]$ predictors,
CLP-CSGM retains $\boldsymbol{b}$ as an explicit measurement penalty during
inference.
It is therefore most relevant when dense logs are informative but incomplete
and sparse target entries should enter that penalty. Log-completion models
fill a missing interval of a wireline curve, geomodel translation fills a
property image from another image, and direct regression maps logs to porosity
or shale volume
\citep{koeshidayatullah2026timegpt,alfakih2026pix2geomodel,alfakih2025wellloggan,alfakih2023northsea}.
CLP-CSGM is the relevant formulation when those dense logs are already
available and a few observations of the target property enter the
reconstructed window through that penalty. Recent foundation-model work also
investigates zero-shot transfer of well-log predictions across geological
basins \citep{koeshidayatullah2026timegpt}. That setting provides a broader
transfer evaluation than the present cross-well benchmark, which uses F03-4 as
a single held-out well. Our results characterize sparse target-measurement
assimilation under this limited evaluation protocol and do not establish
basin-agnostic transferability.

The depth profiles in
Figs.~\ref{fig:crosswell_depth_profile} and~\ref{fig:f03_depth} illustrate how
the balance of these information sources affects reconstruction detail in the
displayed examples. The multi-channel cross-well context supports closer
tracking of the broad held-out $V_c$ variations, whereas the deliberately weak
GR-only context produces a smoother F03-4 porosity reconstruction that omits
some local variations. The displayed porosity window shows that both
CLP-CSGM and the direct autoencoder can miss a local depression. These
examples do not establish a uniform advantage across the benchmark. The
attainable detail is governed jointly by the information in $\boldsymbol{u}$,
the adequacy of $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{orig}}$, and the number and placement of
observations in $\boldsymbol{b}$.

The ablations in Section~\ref{subsec:ablation_analysis} drop one piece at a
time. Using only the decoded prediction of the conditional prior, or only the
measurements, raises the
error in most cells, and which piece matters more changes with the number of
training windows. The full Ridge model has lower mean RMSE than
prior-only throughout the cross-well grid and lower mean RMSE than
measurement-only CSGM in seven of nine cells. In the two step-$8$ exceptions,
measurement-only inversion has lower mean RMSE, so the conditional code
$\boldsymbol{z}_0$ is not helpful in those cells. On the F03-4 grid, where gamma ray is the only
dense log, the full model has lower mean RMSE than both ablations at every
tested ratio. The penalty around $\boldsymbol{z}_0$ keeps the search from
drifting, and the measurement term can still correct it.

The sensitivity results in
Section~\ref{subsec:latent_prior_sensitivity} show that the class of $h$
materially changes reconstruction accuracy, and that no single architecture
is uniformly preferable. In the controlled cross-well comparison, the MLP
prior has lower mean RMSE in seven of the nine cells. That advantage becomes
smaller as the number of training windows decreases and is nearly absent at
step $32$, where Ridge has the lower mean at $\rho=0.10$ and $\rho=0.20$.
The unweighted mean RMSE across those nine cells falls from $0.02862$ for
Ridge to $0.02538$ for MLP, a relative reduction of $11.3\%$. On the
controlled F03-4 GR-only grid, Ridge has lower mean RMSE at all five
measurement ratios. These comparisons distinguish the CLP-CSGM framework
from its reference implementation. Ridge remains the primary reported
configuration because it is a fixed linear low-capacity map: the main tables
then use one $h$ in every cell, and $h$ is not chosen after seeing the test
ranking. The MLP results have the same experimental status. This MLP is the
conditional map $h(\boldsymbol{u})$, not the direct regression MLP
$[\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{b}]$. The preferred class depends on the regime.
The class of $h$, the latent dimension, and the regularization should be
selected on validation data for the intended measurements, not by adopting
whichever variant wins on the held-out test set. The experiments establish
this empirical sensitivity to the class of $h$. They do not show that model
capacity, taken alone, causes the differences.

The empirical scope is limited by the construction of the benchmark targets.
In the cross-well experiment, $V_c$ is a processed Larionov-derived curve.
The gamma-ray index between the clean and shale endpoints is linear in gamma
ray. The Larionov map from that index to clay volume is not a straight line.
If gamma ray has been logged, clay volume is obtained from that relation, and
CLP-CSGM is not a substitute for it. Gamma ray is left out of the main input
because the clay-volume curve was computed from it. Without that log the
Larionov relation cannot be evaluated. The task is then to reconstruct the
processed curve from the other dense logs while matching a few known values
of the curve. Recovery of independently acquired clay-volume measurements is
outside this scope. Putting gamma ray back, in
Appendix~\ref{app:crosswell_gr_embargo}, lowers the absolute error and does
not change the ordering of CLP-CSGM Ridge, the direct AE, and
measurement-only CSGM at step $16$. That run checks the channel. It is not
the log suite used for the main comparison.

The F03-4 porosity curve is density-derived, and its sparse values are taken
from that same curve, not from core or laboratory plugs. Density-derived
porosity can be calculated from bulk density together with the hypotheses of
that relation. A sonic-porosity estimate uses another petrophysical relation
and is not necessarily identical to the density-derived porosity. If the bulk
density required by the density-porosity relation has been recorded, that
relation is the appropriate calculation, and CLP-CSGM is not a substitute
for it. Sonic travel time is left out because it follows this processed curve
almost one to one. The density curve behind the porosity was itself predicted
from sonic and gamma ray, so that correlation is not validation against
independent laboratory porosity. The test uses gamma ray as the only dense
log. Its errors do not transfer to a porosity interpretation that also uses
density, neutron, and sonic. The cross-well benchmark removes gamma ray, the
log required by the Larionov relation. The F03-4 benchmark removes sonic
because of this collinearity with the processed curve. Both compare methods
that must reconstruct the processed curve from the remaining logs while
matching a few known values. They are not a contest against Larionov, density
porosity, or sonic porosity on a fully logged well, and they are not a test
against independent core or laboratory measurements.

A possible field analogue is a well where the log required for a conventional
property estimate is missing or unreliable, while a limited number of
independently acquired property measurements are available. The present F3
benchmarks reproduce only the restricted-input and sparse-observation
structure of this situation: their sparse target values are subsampled from
processed curves, not independently acquired measurements. Therefore, these
tests establish a controlled comparison of reconstruction methods rather than
operational validation of that field scenario. The same limit applies to
simpler reconstruction. Section~\ref{subsec:crosswell_results} and
Appendix~\ref{app:crosswell_simple_baselines} report that depth interpolation
has lower full-window RMSE than CLP-CSGM in all nine cross-well
configurations at the reference noise. As the simulated Gaussian
perturbation increases, CLP-CSGM shows a smaller RMSE increase and becomes
lower in most cells, then in every cell. The same regime-dependent pattern
appears against second-difference smoothing, which also regularizes. This is
relative robustness of the complete procedure, with $\lambda$ reselected on
validation at each noise level. The runs do not isolate the decoder, the
conditional prior, or that reselection, and they do not establish recovery
of local detail or operational superiority. The higher noise levels were not
calibrated to field uncertainties. Section~\ref{subsec:crosswell_results}
also states the extra cost: mean time per window and the normalized runtime
diagnostic in Table~\ref{tab:runtime_cost} are different quantities, and the
larger CLP-CSGM time is not justified in general.

The Lapa permeability case
\citep{alvarez2025nonlinear} extends the evaluation to another lithology and
target property, but its small sample size limits generalization. The
laboratory-porosity experiment in Appendix~\ref{app:phi_lab_861} provides a
supplementary assessment on a target not deterministically derived from the
auxiliary logs by our pipeline. CLP-CSGM Ridge consistently improves upon the
decoded conditional prior, but the direct autoencoder achieves lower aggregate
mean RMSE at both measurement ratios. The block-level differences are
compatible with sensitivity to prior mismatch and changes in the auxiliary-log
distribution, although these effects are not causally isolated. The evaluation
is limited to one cored interval and within-window withheld coordinates. Each
test fold still has many overlapping training windows, so the direct map is in
the regime where it was expected to be competitive. Block RMSE and the log
summaries are reported in the appendix. Broader validation requires larger
multi-well datasets, additional log suites, and facies-conditioned settings.


The reported metrics are not stratified by lithofacies, lithological texture,
or formal stratigraphic interval because the available benchmark tables do not
provide independent geological labels at each depth sample. Consequently,
RMSE, MAE, and relative $\ell_2$ error aggregate heterogeneous windows. The
depth profiles provide qualitative audits but no geological performance
breakdowns. Facies-resolved reliability would require independently interpreted
or core-calibrated interval labels, particularly because the adequacy of the
decoder range and the accuracy of the latent prior may vary across geological
regimes.

The recovery interpretation is also conditional.
Proposition~\ref{prop:conditional_local_recovery} relates reconstruction error
to decoder mismatch, latent-prior mismatch, measurement noise, optimization
quality, and local separability, but does not prove that $M_{\Omega}$ satisfies
S-REC for the trained decoder. The diagnostics in
Appendix~\ref{app:separability} evaluate sampled decoder outputs and should not
be interpreted as an operator-level guarantee.

CLP-CSGM incurs iterative inference cost, whereas direct baselines produce
predictions through a forward pass. The runtime results in
Appendix~\ref{app:runtime} show that this additional cost is most justified
when explicit measurement-consistent refinement produces a meaningful accuracy
gain. Faster latent solvers, warm starts, adaptive stopping, or amortized
refinement are natural directions for larger-scale deployment.
